1.  Introduction and Background 
1.1  Background 
Phishing is when an attacker pretends to be a company you trust, so that you click a link or give away 
your details. The Australian Signals Directorate (2023) says that most phishing messages have a link 
inside that looks real but goes to a copy of a bank or a shopping site. 
The link is the place where the attack works or fails. But addresses are hard to read. Attackers put a 
trusted brand name in the wrong part of the address, or make the address very long, or change letters 
into characters that look the same on the screen. 
A web address always have the same structure, which is scheme, host, path and query. This is written 
in RFC 3986 (Berners-Lee, Fielding and Masinter, 2005). Because the structure is fixed, we can 
measure some things from it, for example how long it is, how many dots it has, or if the host is an IP 
address. Sahingoz et al. (2018) showed that a model trained on features like this can separate phishing 
addresses from normal ones, without opening the page. 
1.2  The Problem 
Most people cannot tell which part of a link is the real website. Attackers hide the real domain behind 
extra subdomains, or put it inside a long query string, or use look-alike characters. Checking by hand is 
slow, and phishing messages are written to make you feel in a hurry. Online checkers already exist, but 
they send the address to the server of another company. This is a privacy problem, and it also needs 
internet. 
1.3  Our Solution 
We will build DTDLINK (Detect The Deception Links). It is a web app that check a link completely 
offline. The user pastes an address, the app reads it as text, calculates a set of features, and a trained 
model predicts how likely it is phishing. The app never opens the link and never sends it anywhere. 
The result is a trust score from 0 to 100, a label like “Likely phishing”, and the list of signals behind that 
score. A second page shows the dataset and how good the model is. DTDLINK is only a learning tool. It 
will never say a link is safe, and every result tells the user to check with the real organisation if the 
request is important. 
We chose this instead of the other options. A blacklist needs internet and it shares user data, and 
downloading the page is not safe. Reading the address offline is private, and three students can finish 
it in twelve weeks with free tools. 
1.4  Project Objectives 
•   Split a web address into its parts without visiting the site. 
•   Write our own code that turns a raw address into features we can measure. 
•   Train and compare at least two models on the dataset. 
•   Show a trust score with an explanation that the user can understand. 
•   Design a simple interface that works on a phone and on a laptop. 
•   Keep proper documentation, meeting minutes and test evidence.

6.  Risk Management 
6.1  How We Manage Risk 
A risk only goes on the register if we can say what causes it, what happens, and what is the result. We 
follow five steps, based on ISO 31000 (International Organization for Standardization, 2018): 
•   Find. We bring risks to the weekly meeting and go through the WBS to ask what could stop each 
package. 
•   Rate. Likelihood and impact from 1 to 5, multiplied together. 
•   Plan. Every medium and high risk gets an owner, a way to prevent it, and a backup plan. 
•   Watch. Owners report every week, and a risk that really happens becomes an issue on the board. 
•   Record. The register is kept in the repository, with the date when each risk was closed. 
6.2  Rating Scales 
Table 9. Likelihood and impact scales 
Score Likelihood Impact 
1 Very unlikely in twelve weeks Small; absorbed in spare time 
2 Could happen once Minor; under two days of rework 
3 Might happen Moderate; a task slips inside its float 
4 Probably will happen Major; a deadline is at risk 
5 Almost certain Severe; we cannot finish something as planned 
 
Likelihood times impact gives the score. 1 to 5 is low and we check it at each milestone, 6 to 12 is 
medium and we check it every two weeks, 15 to 25 is high and we check it every week.

7.  Monitoring and Control 
7.1  Tracking Progress 
We use a shared board with five columns: To Do, In Progress, Review, Blocked and Done. Every task 
has an owner, a due date, the WBS code, and a note about what proof shows that it is finished. A task 
can only move to Done after a second person check that proof. This rule stops us from saying that 
things are finished when they are not. 
In the weekly meeting we compare our progress with Table 7, we count how much float is left, we 
review the risks, and we agree what to do about anything that is late. Every meeting is written as 
minutes in one day, and we take turns to be the minute taker. A decision made in a chat message does 
not count until it is in the minutes. 
7.2  Change Control 
Any change to the scope, the requirements, the schedule or the design must go through a Change 
Control Board. The board looks at the effect on scope, effort and timeline, and then it approves, 
rejects or delays the change. The board is the three of us, and the tutor is the outside stakeholder. 
Nobody has more than two roles, so one person cannot approve a change alone.

9.3  Nielsen’s Ten Usability Heuristics 
We used Nielsen ten heuristics when we designed the screens, and after that as a checklist before the 
user testing (Nielsen, 1994). 
Table 15. Nielsen’s heuristics in our design 
Heuristic Where you can see it 
1. Show system status The button changes while working, the offline badge sits in every header, and the 
result shows score, label and confidence together. 
2. Everyday language We say “web address”, “trust score” and “signals”, not URI, probability and features. 
3. Let users undo Clear empties the box; Check another address returns to the start. 
4. Be consistent Same layout and risk wording everywhere; a colour always means the same thing. 
5. Stop errors early The address is always only text, and Analyse stays disabled until the address is valid 
(Figure 7). 
6. Show, don’t make them 
remember 
The part that causes each warning is highlighted inside the address (Figure 5). 
7. Suit beginners and regulars Example links for a first try; paste and press Enter for regular users. 
8. Keep it simple One box and one button on the main page; statistics live elsewhere. 
9. Help people fix mistakes Errors say what is wrong, show an example, and keep the text that the user typed 
(Figure 7). 
10. Provide help How It Works explains the signals, the score bands and the limits, and you can open 
it from every page (Figure 8).

9.4  Shneiderman’s Eight Golden Rules 
We used Shneiderman eight rules together with Nielsen (Shneiderman et al., 2018). 
Table 16. Shneiderman’s golden rules in our design 
Rule Where you can see it 
1. Be consistent One layout everywhere; risk words and colours never change meaning. 
2. Suit different users Examples for beginners, keyboard for regulars; every colour also has a label. 
3. Give useful feedback Checking as you type, a working state on the button, a confidence figure. 
4. Design a clear ending The result closes with a label and a “What to do next” list. 
5. Prevent errors Opening the link is the dangerous action, so the address is never clickable. 
6. Let users undo Clear, Check another address and Back all return to a known state. 
7. Keep the user in control We give the evidence and let the user decide, and say when we are unsure. 
8. Reduce memory load Address, score, label and signals are all on one screen. 
 
9.5  Colours and Accessibility 
We only use five colours. Navy #0F2743 for headings, blue #2563C9 for actions, light grey-blue 
#F4F6FA for backgrounds, red #B3261E for high risk and amber #B26B00 for medium risk. Red and 
amber mean risk and nothing else, so when red appears it means something. Every risk colour also has 
a word next to it, so the meaning never depend only on the colour. The score always comes together 
with a label and a next step, because a number alone makes people invent their own cut-off. 
We want WCAG 2.2 Level AA on the main screens (World Wide Web Consortium, 2023). That means 
contrast of at least 4.5:1, keyboard access with a visible focus ring, labelled input boxes, and a layout 
that still works at 375 px. We will test the prototype with five students in Week 4 and fix anything that 
stops them from finishing the main task.
